# Era groupings and why the boundaries fall where they do

Internal note. The five eras are this project's own groupings, not an official Vatican classification, and the site says so in the legend notes and on the "What is a pope?" page.

The boundaries follow the section headings of Britannica's "Papacy" article. Each boundary falls between two pontificates, so every pope belongs to exactly one era. The roster rows on either side of each boundary were checked against the Vatican list.

## Early Church (c. 30s to 590), rows 1 to 63

St Peter to Pelagius II. Britannica heading: "The early papacy: from St. Peter to 590 ce".

**Rationale.** Opens with St Peter (roster 1). Ends with Pelagius II (d. 7 Feb 590, roster 63); the boundary is the election of Gregory I in 590, whom the Catholic Encyclopedia (quoting Milman) calls 'the real father of the medieval papacy': he negotiated with the Lombards when Byzantine help failed, sent the mission to England and administered the Church's estates and Rome's civic needs, foreshadowing papal temporal power. Britannica's first section runs 'from St. Peter to 590 ce'. Alternative: Duffy's first chapter, 'Upon this Rock', ends earlier, with Leo the Great (461).

**Public summary.** From St Peter to the late sixth century, the bishops of Rome led a young Church through persecution, the conversion of the empire and the fall of the West. Many are honoured as saints, though records of their lives are often slight.

Sources: Papacy (https://www.britannica.com/topic/papacy); Pope St. Gregory I ('the Great') (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06780a.htm); Review of Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes, 4th ed. (2014) (https://catholicbooksreview.org/2015/duffy.html); Supreme Pontiffs (roster) (https://www.vatican.va/content/vatican/en/holy-father.html)

## Medieval (590 to 1303), rows 64 to 193

St Gregory I (the Great) to Boniface VIII. Britannica heading: "The medieval papacy: from 590 to 1303".

**Rationale.** Opens with Gregory I (elected 590, consecrated 3 Sept 590; roster 64). Ends with Boniface VIII (roster 193): his conflict with Philip IV of France (Clericis laicos 1296, Unam Sanctam 1302), his seizure at Anagni on 7 Sept 1303 and his death on 11 Oct 1303 mark the end of high-medieval papal claims. After the brief pontificate of Benedict XI, Clement V (elected 1305) settled in France and the popes resided at Avignon c. 1309-1377. Britannica: 'from 590 to 1303'. Alternative: Duffy splits this span into 'Between Two Empires' and 'Set Above Nations' (the latter beginning with the Gregorian reform).

**Public summary.** From Gregory the Great to Boniface VIII, popes became major figures in a Christian Europe, crowning emperors, reforming the Church and calling crusades and councils. The era closes amid a bitter clash with the French crown.

Sources: Papacy (https://www.britannica.com/topic/papacy); Pope St. Gregory I ('the Great') (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06780a.htm); Pope Boniface VIII (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02662a.htm); Pope Clement V (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04020a.htm); Avignon papacy (https://www.britannica.com/event/Avignon-papacy); Review of Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes, 4th ed. (2014) (https://catholicbooksreview.org/2015/duffy.html)

## Renaissance and Reformation (1303 to 1565), rows 194 to 224

Bl. Benedict XI to Pius IV. Britannica heading: "The Renaissance and Reformation papacy: from 1303 to 1565".

**Rationale.** Opens with Benedict XI (elected Oct 1303; roster 194), the first pope after Boniface VIII's death, on the eve of the Avignon residence (Clement V; c. 1309-1377). Ends with Pius IV (d. 9 Dec 1565; roster 224), who brought the Council of Trent (opened 13 Dec 1545) to its close on 4 Dec 1563 and confirmed its decrees by the bull Benedictus Deus (26 Jan 1564). Britannica: 'from 1303 to 1565'. Alternative: Duffy treats the Renaissance and Reformation in a single chapter, not separated from the late Middle Ages at 1303.

**Public summary.** From Benedict XI to Pius IV, the papacy lived through the Avignon years, the Great Western Schism, Renaissance splendour and the Protestant Reformation. It ends with the Council of Trent, which set the course of Catholic renewal.

Sources: Papacy (https://www.britannica.com/topic/papacy); Pope Boniface VIII (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02662a.htm); Pope Clement V (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04020a.htm); Avignon papacy (https://www.britannica.com/event/Avignon-papacy); Council of Trent (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15030c.htm); Review of Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes, 4th ed. (2014) (https://catholicbooksreview.org/2015/duffy.html)

## Early modern (1566 to 1774), rows 225 to 249

St Pius V to Clement XIV. Britannica heading: "The early modern papacy: from 1566 to 1774".

**Rationale.** Opens with Pius V (elected 7 Jan 1566; roster 225), who enforced the discipline of Trent (the Tridentine catechism, breviary and missal followed under him; dates of those not confirmed from the fetched CE page). Ends with Clement XIV (d. 22 Sept 1774; roster 249), who under pressure from the Bourbon courts suppressed the Society of Jesus by the brief Dominus ac Redemptor, dated 21 July 1773. Britannica: 'from 1566 to 1774'. Alternative: Duffy's fifth chapter, 'The Pope and the People', begins in 1774.

**Public summary.** From Pius V to Clement XIV, popes put the reforms of Trent into practice and sent missionaries across the world, while Catholic monarchs grew ever more powerful. The era ends with the suppression of the Jesuits in 1773.

Sources: Papacy (https://www.britannica.com/topic/papacy); Council of Trent (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15030c.htm); Pope St. Pius V (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12130a.htm); Breve Dominus ac Redemptor (21 July 1773) (https://www.vatican.va/content/clemens-xiv/pt/documents/breve-dominus-ac-redemptor-21-luglio-1773.html); Review of Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes, 4th ed. (2014) (https://catholicbooksreview.org/2015/duffy.html)

## Modern (1775 to today), rows 250 to 267

Pius VI to Leo XIV. Britannica heading: "The modern papacy: from 1775 to the 21st century".

**Rationale.** Opens with Pius VI (elected 15 Feb 1775; roster 250), whose pontificate ran into the French Revolution: he rejected the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1791), was removed from Rome by French forces in Feb 1798 and died in custody at Valence on 29 Aug 1799. Runs to Leo XIV (elected 8 May 2025; roster 267). Britannica: 'from 1775 to the 21st century'. Alternative: Duffy divides this span at 1903 ('The Pope and the People', 1774-1903; 'The Oracles of God', 1903 onward).

**Public summary.** From Pius VI to Leo XIV, the papacy passed through revolution, the loss of the Papal States and two Vatican Councils. Today's popes are spiritual leaders with a worldwide voice, travelling far beyond Rome.

Sources: Papacy (https://www.britannica.com/topic/papacy); Pope Pius VI (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12131a.htm); Review of Eamon Duffy, Saints and Sinners: A History of the Popes, 4th ed. (2014) (https://catholicbooksreview.org/2015/duffy.html); Supreme Pontiffs (roster) (https://www.vatican.va/content/vatican/en/holy-father.html)

# Working papacy eras

Checked 10 October 2026. These are **provisional project navigation groups**, retained from the brief for the initial prototype. They are not an official Vatican classification or a claim that every pontificate has one historically uncontested period label. The Vatican roster supplies the sequence; historical sources explain the transitions. [E1–E7]

## Historical rationale and limitations

**Pelagius II → Gregory I (590).** Gregory's leadership during the Lombard conflicts and his missions to the new European peoples make his accession a useful narrative pivot. Benedict XVI discusses the Byzantine setting, negotiations with the Lombards, and the mission to England. Gregory also belongs to the Fathers of the Church; the boundary must not imply that antiquity ended neatly on his accession. [E2] The Catholic Encyclopedia explicitly records disagreement about the ancient/medieval boundary, including 590 and later alternatives; its own preferred broad scheme uses the end of the seventh century. [E3]

**Boniface VIII → Benedict XI (1303).** This boundary marks the aftermath of Boniface's conflict with Philip IV and the Anagni attack, followed by Benedict's efforts to restore relations with France. [E4] It provides room for the Avignon and schism stories alongside the later Renaissance and Reformation, but the label is broader than its earliest members. A Catholic historical periodization treats 1303 as an internal medieval transition; the Renaissance source discusses classical revival from the late fourteenth century. Do not claim that Benedict XI was a Renaissance pope or that the Renaissance began in 1303. [E3, E5]

**Pius IV → Pius V (1565/1566).** This boundary is a practical shift from the conciliar reform era to its implementation. A Vatican-hosted homily associates Pius V with the Catholic reform desired by Trent and discusses his Missal, Breviary, and catechism. It also calls him a pope of the Italian Renaissance, showing why the project's labels cannot be exclusive historical identities. [E6] Pius IV's whole reign remains in the preceding group; no pontificate is split at the close of Trent or at a calendar year.

**Clement XIV → Pius VI (1774/1775).** Pius VI's reign contains both the late eighteenth-century monarchy setting and the French Revolutionary crisis, including his forced removal from Rome and death in France. Including his whole pontificate in the modern group preserves the project's one-era-per-person rule. [E7] This is an editorial boundary at 1775, not a claim that the French Revolution occurred then. Catholic historians also use 1789, among other divisions. [E3]

No consulted Catholic source offers the exact five names and all four boundaries as a single authoritative scheme. The Catholic Encyclopedia's broad three-period scheme is a useful comparison, but its subdivisions and dates differ and it concerns general Church history rather than this map's five papal illustrations. Retain the brief's five groups with the above qualifications. Changing them later requires a documented editorial decision and synchronized data, legend, markers, and timeline changes.

## Internal numbered source register

E-prefixed numbers are local research keys, to be mapped to the unified source numbers in `data/popes.json` during integration. They never appear in the public interface. All were read using the connected web research tool on 10 October 2026.

1. **E1 — Pontiffs, The Holy See.** <https://www.vatican.va/content/vatican/en/holy-father.html>. Table rows 63–64, 193–194, 224–225, 249–250, and 267 support order, transition dates, and current endpoint. The table classifies by century; it does not prescribe the five project eras.
2. **E2 — Benedict XVI, General Audience, “Saint Gregory the Great (1),” 28 May 2008.** <https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2008/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20080528.html>. Paragraphs on the Lombards, the Byzantine emperor, new European peoples, and Augustine of Canterbury support Gregory's transitional narrative. This is papal catechesis, not an era taxonomy.
3. **E3 — Johann Peter Kirsch, “Ecclesiastical History,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 7, New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1910.** <https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07365a.htm>. “Universal history of the Church,” boundary discussion and chronological arrangement: competing 590/680/692 boundaries, Boniface VIII/1303 as a medieval subdivision, and the French Revolution/1789. The page records a 1 June 1910 nihil obstat and Archbishop John Cardinal Farley's imprimatur. This is historical local ecclesiastical approval, not Vatican endorsement of the proposed periodization or later website edits.
4. **E4 — Martin Augustine Waldron, “Pope Benedict XI,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 2, New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1907.** <https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02429c.htm>. Paragraphs on the 1303 Anagni attack, election after Boniface's death, and restored relations with France. The page records local ecclesiastical approval; its historical narrative is supplementary to the current Vatican roster.
5. **E5 — William Barry, “The Renaissance,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 12, New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1911.** <https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12765b.htm>. Opening definition dates classical revival from the late fourteenth century; discussion of the commonly assigned height (1453–1527) shows why 1303 is not the literal start. The article records a 1 June 1911 nihil obstat and Archbishop John Cardinal Farley's imprimatur. Treat its dated interpretations critically.
6. **E6 — Cardinal Angelo Sodano, Mass of Thanksgiving on the fifth centenary of the birth of Pope St Pius V, homily, 18 January 2004.** <https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/secretariat_state/2004/documents/rc_seg-st_20040118_sodano-alessandria_en.html>. Opening and “On the Chair of Peter” discuss Trent's reform and the Missal, Breviary, and catechism; the opening also calls Pius V a pope of the Renaissance. Its 17 January accession reference differs from the roster's paired 7/17 January dates; the roster governs the sequence display.
7. **E7 — Michael Ott, “Pope Pius VI,” The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 12, New York: Robert Appleton Company, 1911.** <https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12131a.htm>. Paragraphs on the French Revolution and his 1798 removal and 1799 death support inclusion of the Revolutionary crisis within his whole pontificate. The page records a 1 June 1911 nihil obstat and Archbishop John Cardinal Farley's imprimatur; the current Vatican roster governs accession dates.
